Why Gotham looks like a road sign

The design system that tells millions of people where to go, every day, without them noticing

Gotham is a map of warnings, so it borrows the look of Britain's best-known warnings: its road signs. They were designed in the late 1950s and early 1960s by Jock Kinneir and his former student Margaret Calvert, and they are still in use today.

A short history

  1. Before 1957Britain's signs were a patchwork, with different shapes, colours and lettering depending on who had put them up.
  2. 1957With the first motorways coming, the Ministry of Transport set up the Anderson Committee. It appointed Kinneir as designer, with Calvert as his assistant.
  3. 1958The new motorway signs were tested on the Preston bypass, now part of the M6. A year later they went up on the M1.
  4. July 1963A second group, the Worboys Committee, published its report on signs for every other road, again with Kinneir and Calvert as designers.
  5. 1 January 1965The new system became law.
  6. 2016Calvert was appointed OBE for services to typography and road safety.

Shape tells you what kind of sign it is

The system follows an international agreement from 1949, so a driver can read the type of a sign before reading what's on it.

Primary-route signs put the road number in yellow, which is where the yellow "UK" on Gotham's own sign comes from.

Letters built for reading at speed

Kinneir and Calvert drew their own typeface, called Transport. It's rounder and friendlier than the harsh capitals used abroad, with small details that make letters easier to tell apart, such as the curled tail on the lowercase l and t. Signs use upper and lower case, because a word's outline is easier to recognise at a glance than a block of capitals.

Calvert also drew many of the pictograms. The girl leading a boy on the "children crossing" sign is based on a photo of Calvert herself as a child, and the cow on the farm-animals sign was Patience, from her relatives' farm in Warwickshire.

Where you'll see it in Gotham

  • Each tested hotspot is a warning triangle, numbered by rank.
  • The name sits on a motorway sign, with its white inset border.
  • Harm runs from primary-route green through yellow to warning red.
  • Notes on the map, like where data is missing, are small white local signs.
